Summary — Unit 0: Karl Buzengeiger’s Vorrede (pp. III–XVI)¶

Philosophical & Methodological Framing of Feuerbach’s 1822 Monograph

  1. Dual Aspect of Mathematics (pp. III–IV): Karl Buzengeiger opens with a classical distinction between mathematics as deductive science (Wissenschaft — exemplified above all by Euclid’s Elements) and mathematics as heuristic art (Kunst — exemplified by Greek geometric analysis in Euclid's Data, Apollonius as restored by Robert Simson, and the arithmetical Arithmetica of Diophantus).

  2. Critique of Combinatorial Notation & Factorials (p. XV): Reflecting on early nineteenth-century German mathematics, Buzengeiger praises analytical factorials (Fakultäten, studied by Kramp and Bessel) for evaluating intractable integrals while sharply criticizing the Hindenburg combinatorial school’s “repulsive and unserviceable wilderness of notation” (Zeichenwust). By contrast, Feuerbach’s monograph employs clean, symmetric algebraic-trigonometric notation in the spirit of Euler and L'Huilier.

  3. The Inexhaustible Plane Triangle (p. XVI): Answering the fear that elementary geometry had been exhausted since antiquity, Buzengeiger points directly to his twenty-one-year-old pupil’s discovery: even the rectilinear triangle—the simplest figure in plane geometry—still harbors unsuspected structural theorems of the first rank when investigated by a “thoroughly systematic mind” (einen sehr systematischen Kopf).
